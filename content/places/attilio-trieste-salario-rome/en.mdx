Somebody was renovating a shopfront on Via di Villa Chigi and pulled a 19th-century portrait out from behind a wall. Nobody working on the job knew who the man in the frame was. A little digging turned up a name — Attilio Rendano — and a maybe: possibly a relative of the pianist and composer Alfonso Rendano, never confirmed past that. Rather than toss the painting or hang it in storage, the people opening the bar in that shopfront named the place after him. That's the whole origin story, and it's a better one than most bars invent on purpose.

Attilio opened in late March 2026 in Trieste-Salario, a quiet residential quarter north of Villa Ada that has approximately none of the bar-crawl density of Trastevere or San Lorenzo. It reads, from the street, like a neighborhood bar — vintage fittings, a room built for lingering rather than turnover. What it actually is, per [Puntarella Rossa's own reporting on the opening](https://www.puntarellarossa.it/2026/04/02/nuove-aperture-a-roma-aprile-2026-ristoranti-pizzerie-bar/), is closer to a wine bar with a neighborhood bar's manners: a cellar built mostly around natural wine, craft beer from Abruzzo's Birrificio La Monna, and — the thing worth crossing the quarter for — a Champagne list weighted toward récoltant-manipulant houses.

## What récoltant-manipulant actually means, and why it's the point here

Most Champagne on most lists in Rome comes from the big négociant houses — Champagne producers who buy fruit from growers across the region and blend it into a consistent house style, vintage over vintage. Récoltant-manipulant (RM) is the opposite model: a grower who farms their own vines and disgorges their own bottles, so what's in the glass reflects one specific plot of Champagne rather than a house recipe. It's a smaller, more variable, more expensive-to-source category, and building a bar's identity around it — rather than treating it as one line on a longer list — is a real commitment, not a marketing line. [Lieviti Digitali's independent write-up on the opening](https://www.lievitidigitali.com/attilio-roma/) describes the space in almost the same breath as "a lounge bar with a wine cellar" first and a neighborhood bar second, which tracks: the drink is doing the talking here.

The kitchen is built to support that, not distract from it. The menu runs small and shareable — babaganoush, eggplant parmigiana, picchiapò (Rome's own boiled-beef-in-tomato dish, a genuine Roman regional plate rather than a generic Italian one), vitello tonnato, baccalà mantecato, polpette, and a pastrami club sandwich that's the closest thing on the list to an outlier. Ingredients are sourced from the neighborhood's own market, butcher and fishmonger where the sourcing allows it, which is the kind of detail that either means something or means nothing depending on whether a place actually does it consistently — worth asking about on a slow night rather than taking on faith.

## Reading the room, and what's actually confirmed

Attilio's own Instagram bio reads "Qui si riparano ricordi" — "here, memories are repaired" — which lines up with the portrait story a little too neatly to be an accident; someone clearly built the whole identity around the found painting rather than bolting the story on after the fact. The venue keeps a loose events calendar beyond the bar itself: Puntarella Rossa's piece mentions occasional live music and tastings, plus a recurring informal gathering the venue calls a monthly press review — journalists and guests talking current events and food culture over a glass. No dated 2026 edition of that gathering was confirmed independently at the time of writing, so treat it as a recurring house habit rather than a bookable date; check the bar's own Instagram (@attilio_bar) before planning a visit around it.

Google's listing for Attilio Bar shows a strong early read — 4.6 stars across 31 reviews as of this writing, useful mainly for the specifics reviewers volunteer rather than the number itself. One reviewer singled out the parmigiana by name and called the pricing fair for the quality; another flagged the wine, sparkling and beer selection specifically rather than the cocktails, which matches the bar's own stated emphasis. No pattern of complaints has surfaced yet — worth noting the venue is young enough (open since March 2026) that a longer review history hasn't had time to accumulate, so treat this as an early, favorable but thin read rather than a settled verdict. It's open nightly, 18:00 to midnight, with no day off — a genuine seven-night bar in a quarter that mostly goes quiet after dinner.

Trieste-Salario doesn't have much company for a bar like this yet, which cuts both ways: nowhere nearby to bar-hop before or after, but also none of the competition for attention that would come with a Trastevere or Monti address. If récoltant-manipulant Champagne and a Roman regional plate menu sound worth a detour from the centro, this is currently the reason to make one.
