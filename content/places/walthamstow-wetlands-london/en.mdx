I'm contractually grumpy about cities, so believe me when I say this: the best thing about London is that you can leave it without leaving it. Fifteen minutes from the centre, off the Victoria line, there are **160 hectares of open water and reedbed** where the loudest thing is a tern having an argument. It's free. Take the train.

[Walthamstow Wetlands](https://www.wildlondon.org.uk/walthamstow-wetlands-nature-reserve) is the thing almost no other capital has: a **working reservoir complex** — ten basins still supplying drinking water to 3.5 million people — that is simultaneously an internationally important nature reserve, run by the London Wildlife Trust with Thames Water and the borough, and open to anyone who walks in. Most cities hide their water infrastructure behind fences. London fenced this one too, for a century and a half — then opened the gate in 2017 and discovered it had been keeping a wilderness.

## What's actually there

Peregrine falcons, kingfishers, grey herons, great crested grebes doing their absurd courtship dance in spring, and in the reeds the general seething business of a wetland that has had 150 years to get on with itself. It's also **the largest fishery in London**, which tells you what's under the surface. The skyline never quite goes away — you watch a heron lift off with the Blackhorse Road flats behind it — and that's not a flaw, that's the whole argument: this is what infrastructure looks like when something is allowed to live on it.

The walking is flat, open and honest — this is big-sky reservoir walking, not woodland — and on a windy day it feels closer to an estuary than to anything ten minutes from a tube station has a right to.

## The rules, which I will now state without softening

**You cannot swim here.** Not "discouraged" — prohibited, and the Trust's reasoning is the correct kind: deep, cold working-reservoir water with hidden dangers. This is not a wild-swimming spot and no amount of Instagram will make it one; Near's swimming recommendation for this city is <NearLink slug="brockwell-lido-london">a lido built for exactly that purpose</NearLink>.

**No dogs, except assistance dogs.** The birds were here first and ground-nesting species don't negotiate.

**The hours are shorter than you expect.** Open daily from 9:30am; last entry is mid-afternoon — 3:45pm on winter weekdays, a little later in summer — and the gates genuinely close. No barbecues, no alcohol, no foraging; cyclists and joggers stay on the marked paths.

And one timing note that is true the week I'm writing this: the Trust's own page says paths 1-2-3 and 4-5 have been closed for electricity-pylon upgrade works, **"expected to be completed by the end of August"** — which is now. If your plan depends on those specific stretches, check the Trust's page the day before rather than trusting anyone's cached copy, including this one.

## How to do it right

Blackhorse Road station (Victoria line and Overground), ten minutes' walk to the Forest Road entrance by the Engine House, where there's a café that closes half an hour before the site does. Go on a weekday morning if you can; go in winter deliberately, when the duck numbers are stupid and the light is low and sideways. Then walk out the far end into the Lea Valley and keep going — the reserve is the front door to a green corridor that runs further than your legs will.

## Why go

Because the pitch for London keeps being its rooms — clubs, halls, bookshops — and the honest counter-argument is that the city also contains a functioning wetland the size of a small town that asks nothing of you but a tap-in fare and an early start.
