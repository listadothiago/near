Bologna has carried the nickname "La Rossa" — the Red City — for most of a century, for reasons that run through electoral politics, a long tradition of cooperative and mutual-aid organizing, and a persistent, generations-deep habit of groups of people occupying empty buildings and turning them into something the city ends up using anyway. Vag61 is one of the longer-running examples still standing. It didn't start where it is now: in 2003, a group occupied a disused state-owned building at Via Azzo Gardino 61 — hence the name, "Vag," from the address, plus "61" — and ran it as an open, self-managed space until that site was cleared. Rather than dissolving, the collective moved to Via Paolo Fabbri 110, in the Cirenaica neighborhood on Bologna's near-east side, where it has operated continuously since and, per its own account, seen off more than one attempt to shut it down.

## What "self-managed" actually means here

It's worth being precise rather than romantic about what Vag61 is. Its own "Chi siamo" page describes it in blunt terms: antifascist, antisexist, antimilitarist, oriented around solidarity and mutual aid, run through collective decision-making with no owner and no hierarchy in the ordinary business sense. That's the group's own framing, not this piece editorializing on top of it — it's the language they choose to describe themselves, and it's worth taking at face value rather than softening into vaguer "community space" copy or inflating into something more provocative than the source material supports. In practice it functions less like a single venue and more like an umbrella over a cluster of ongoing projects sharing one address: CentroDoc, a documentation archive focused on social movements; Fondo Roversi, a collected archive; SMK Videofactory, a self-produced video and media collective; a Doposcuola, a free after-school support program for neighborhood kids; and Brigata Cucinieri, the volunteer kitchen crew that feeds most of what happens on-site.

## The Tuesday you can actually plan around

The single most concrete, repeatable reason to show up is the Mercatino di Campi Aperti — a weekly organic farmers' market that's been running out of Vag61's courtyard for over a decade, bringing in small producers selling fruit, vegetables, cheese, honey, bread, wine, and household basics like ecological cleaning products, all sold directly by the people who make them, cutting out the usual retail markup. It runs Tuesday evenings from around 6pm, and for years it's paired with a free "aperitivo musicale" — reggae and hip-hop, historically curated under names like Magazzino Ganjalabria — turning what could be a plain produce stand into an actual weekly social occasion with a soundtrack and no cover charge. That combination, market plus music plus zero admission fee, is the clearest evidence that Vag61 functions as ongoing cultural programming rather than a static political statement: it's a recurring appointment locals can build a Tuesday around, not a building you'd only ever pass by.

## Reading the neighborhood

Via Paolo Fabbri sits just off Via San Donato, in Cirenaica — a district whose streets were renamed after Italian Resistance partisans following the Second World War, replacing an earlier colonial-era naming scheme, and which grew up as one of Bologna's first working-class extensions beyond the medieval walls. Via San Donato itself runs out from Porta San Donato, the old city gate that also gives its name to the university quarter it opens onto, so the geography lines up cleanly with the politics: this is university-adjacent Bologna, historically dense with student housing and left-wing organizing, and Vag61 sits squarely inside that continuity rather than being some outlier plopped into a random residential street. None of this requires exaggeration to be interesting — a farmers' market with a soundsystem, run for free by volunteers out of a twice-relocated squat, in a neighborhood named for partisans, is specific enough on its own.

Anyone treating Bologna as strictly the tortellini-and-porticoes postcard is going to miss an entire, still-active layer of the city that a lot of actual Bologna residents consider just as central to what the place is.
