Berlin's queer nightlife reputation runs almost entirely through one word: Berghain. It's not wrong, exactly — the club is real and the door is real — but it's a postcard version of a city whose actual queer infrastructure has less to do with a converted power plant in Friedrichshain and more to do with Kreuzberg-Kotti, Schöneberg, and a lake in a forest that don't need a wristband story to prove they matter. SO36, a block off Kottbusser Tor, is the clearest case: a 48-year-old punk venue that's also spent 28 of those years hosting the queer Turkish and Arabic community on the last Saturday of nearly every month, with almost no tourist-brochure attention to show for it.

## A building that keeps getting repurposed

Oranienstraße 190 didn't start as a punk club. Per SO36's own history and Wikipedia's account, the site opened as a beer garden in 1861, later became a cinema, and was burnt out in 1966 — the kind of building that had already lived two lives before it opened as a music venue in August 1978. Artist Martin Kippenberger took over its management in 1979 and pushed it toward crossover programming between punk, New Wave, and visual art, and the club spent the years after cemented as ground zero for Berlin's punk and squatter scene: Dead Kennedys, Killing Joke, New Model Army, and homegrown acts like Die Toten Hosen all played there, and The Berliner's own 40th-anniversary retrospective treats it as a fixture that outlasted the scene that built it. It's still run as a non-profit — Sub Opus 36 e.V. — by a collective that describes its mission in explicitly anti-sexist, anti-racist, anti-antisemitic, and anti-homophobic terms, not as a marketing line but as the operating premise for who gets booked and what happens on stage.

## Gayhane, 28 years and counting

The other half of SO36's story runs on a completely different rhythm. GAYHANE — QueerOriental Dancefloor started in 1997, founded by Turkish performer Fatma Souad, and has run close to monthly ever since; SO36's own ticket shop marked a "28 Jahre GAYHANE" anniversary night on 31 January 2026, and DJ Ipek Ipekcioglu — a resident of the night since the beginning — is still on the lineup alongside DJs mikki_p, Aziza A, and Amr Hammer. The premise hasn't changed in almost three decades: Turkish, Arabic, Greek, and Hebrew pop on the speakers, the room styled as an "oriental festival tent," and the night traditionally closing out with the halay, the Middle Eastern and Turkish circle dance that gives the party its full name — House of Halay. It's aimed at, in SO36's own description, "queers, lesbians, transpeople, gays" from the Turkish and Arabic diaspora specifically, a scene that doesn't get built by accident and doesn't survive 28 years by being a novelty booking.

SO36's own listings confirm four Gayhane dates through the end of 2026: 26 September, 31 October, 28 November, and 26 December, each starting at 10pm, with tickets running €15 at the door and €8 with a Berlin Pass. That's a longer runway than most club nights anywhere manage to publish in advance, and it's worth planning around rather than showing up on a random Saturday and hoping.

## Why this matters more than another Berghain writeup

The "techno cathedral" framing of queer Berlin that dominates travel coverage skips past the fact that a lot of the city's actual queer social infrastructure is unglamorous, neighborhood-rooted, and has been running quietly since before most of the clubs currently trending on Berlin listicles existed. SO36 sitting at the center of both stories — the punk landmark and the queer Turkish-Arabic institution — isn't a coincidence. Kreuzberg-Kotti has been a place where immigrant communities, punks, and queer scenes overlapped and organized in the same buildings for decades, and Gayhane is what that overlap actually produced: not a theme night bolted onto a famous venue's calendar, but one of the longest-running queer parties in the city, on its own terms, still selling out a room that's been standing at Oranienstraße 190 since long before anyone called this neighborhood a scene.
