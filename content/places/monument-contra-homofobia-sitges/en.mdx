The nearest thing Sitges has to a thesis statement is 110 centimetres tall, made of iron sheet painted pink, and pointing downwards at a cement block on the seafront promenade.

The inverted triangle is the badge homosexual prisoners were made to wear in Nazi camps, reclaimed by activists in the 1970s, and choosing it for a monument in a resort town in 2006 was a decision to make the place's queer history read as history rather than as a brochure.

## What the date on the plaque means

The plaque says: *Sitges contra la homofòbia / Mai més / 5 d'octubre de 1996-2006.* Sitges against homophobia. Never again.

The 1996 half needs unpacking, and the sources do not fully agree, which is worth saying rather than smoothing over. [The town council's own sculpture-park catalogue](https://www.sitges.cat/serveis/cultura/parc-escultoric/18-sitges-contra-lhomofobia) records only that the monument marks the tenth anniversary of homophobic incidents in Sitges on 5 October 1996. [PinkNews, reporting the unveiling in 2006](https://www.thepinknews.com/2006/10/11/monument-against-homophobia-in-sitges/), describes a demonstration ten years earlier against a plan to record gay men on the beach at night, and violent counter-protest — eggs and stones — against the people demonstrating. Fuller and grimmer accounts circulate in guidebooks and listicles; none of them is sourced well enough to repeat here.

What is not in dispute is the response. The town did not quietly host a plaque paid for by a bar association. It put the thing on the main seafront and catalogued it in the municipal sculpture park, which is a very specific kind of civic statement.

## When to actually stand in front of it

It takes two minutes to look at and it is free, so the honest advice is about when rather than whether. The monument sits on the open promenade with the sea behind it and no shade, and in high summer at midday it reads as street furniture you walk past on the way to a beach. Early morning or the hour before sunset, with the promenade half-empty, it reads as what it is.

Don't miss the base. It is a working memorial, not a finished one — people leave things there, as the 2016 photograph above shows, and what is at its foot on any given day is more of the point than the sculpture above it.

It is a ten-minute walk from here up to <NearLink slug="parrots-pub-sitges">Parrots</NearLink> and the bar strip, and doing those two in that order — monument first, terrace after — is a better afternoon than doing it the other way round.
