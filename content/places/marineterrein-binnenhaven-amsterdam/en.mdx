I've spent years telling people not to jump in Amsterdam's canals, and I'm not taking it back. But as of April 2025, there's finally one specific rectangle of water in the city center where you're not breaking any rule by getting in, and it's worth being precise about which one, because it is exactly one.

## What actually happened, and when

The Binnenhaven — the old inner harbor basin on the Marineterrein, the former naval base tucked behind Amsterdam Centraal between Kattenburg and the Scheepvaartmuseum — was designated Amsterdam Centrum's first official swimming location. The province of Noord-Holland signed off, Waternet (the regional water authority) announced it on 20 February 2025, and the status became active at the start of that year's swim season in April. That's not a marketing claim I'm taking on faith — Waternet, Waterschap AGV, IamExpat, and NL Times all reported the same timeline independently, which is about as verified as a municipal water designation gets. This water had already been informally used for swimming for years before that — what changed in 2025 is the paperwork and the monitoring, not people suddenly discovering it existed.

Getting official status wasn't a rubber stamp. It followed actual testing: the seabed was inspected, gaps between the dock walls and the harbor floor were filled in, and water quality was measured repeatedly over multiple seasons before anyone signed off on it. A "Dobbernatuur" project has also floated experimental nature elements in the harbor to study their effect on water quality — this isn't a beach that got fenced off and branded, it's a working industrial dock that had to earn the designation.

## What's there, and — more to the point — what isn't

The access point is a vlonder — a 2.5-meter-wide composite boardwalk connecting the harbor's quays, built from a non-slip recyclable material, with a section that doubles as a swimming pontoon. That's the whole facility. There's no lifeguard. There are no changing rooms, no showers, nothing you'd associate with a managed swimming area. Waternet says it plainly: swimming here is at your own risk, same as every wild-swim spot before the sign went up, except now the water itself is being watched.

That monitoring is the actual upgrade. Water quality and temperature readings are posted in real time on marineterrein.nl and on digital screens on-site, and — this is the detail that matters if you're planning to actually get in — the floating dock closes for 72 hours after heavy rainfall, because Amsterdam's sewer system can overflow into open water during storms. If you show up two days after a downpour and the pontoon's closed, that's the system working, not a bureaucratic overreaction. Swimming is possible here year-round, not just in summer, but "possible" and "comfortable" are different claims — this is North Sea-climate open water with zero temperature control, so treat "year-round" as a fact about legality, not an invitation to a January dip without knowing what you're doing.

## Where you still can't swim — said plainly, because most guides won't

Here's the part every breathless "Amsterdam now has a swimming spot!" post skips: this designation covers one harbor basin. It does not extend to the actual canals — the Prinsengracht, the Herengracht, the water that shows up on every postcard. Those remain unofficial and, per Amsterdam's own health authority (GGD Amsterdam) and city police, genuinely risky to swim in: heavy boat traffic makes swimmers hard to see, the water quality isn't monitored the way an official spot's is, and there's submerged debris — including, unglamorously, actual bicycles — that you cannot see from the surface. Police have handed out fines around €160 for canal swimming, and it's not enforced as theater; it's enforced because boats and swimmers share water that was never designed for both. The Marineterrein basin got singled out precisely because someone did the work of testing and fixing it. The grachtengordel didn't, and nobody's proposed to.

Zoom out and Amsterdam now has ten officially designated outdoor swim spots total — Marineterrein Binnenhaven is the only one actually inside the city center; the rest (Sloterparkbad, the Amsterdamse Bos's Grote Vijver, Strand IJburg, Gaasperplas, and others) sit further out in parks and purpose-built recreation areas. If you want to check current conditions anywhere in the country before getting in, zwemwater.nl is the actual national registry, not a guess.

## Who this is for

If what you want is a real swim, in real open water, five minutes from Centraal Station, with nobody watching you and nothing but a status screen telling you whether the water's clean today — this is genuinely that, and it's new, and it's legal, which up until last year it technically wasn't. If what you want is warm water, a changing room, and a lifeguard whistle if you get into trouble, go to Sloterparkbad instead and don't pretend the harbor owes you that. I'd rather tell you the dock closes after rain and there's no shower than let you find that out standing there dripping with nowhere to towel off.

For a drink after, walk it off toward the Zeedijk and duck into <NearLink slug="cafe-t-mandje-amsterdam">Café 't Mandje</NearLink> — it's the kind of unpretentious, been-here-forever room that suits showing up salty and unbothered, which is more than you can say for most places near a train station.
