You ring the buzzer. There is no sign. There has never been a sign. If you're standing on Frith Street looking for a hotel-shaped building with a hotel-shaped logo, you will walk past it, because the whole point of Hazlitt's is that it looks like exactly what it is: three Georgian townhouses that happen to have a very good lock and a very tired night porter behind door number 6. Condé Nast Traveller put it plainly enough that the hotel now quotes it on its own site — "there's nothing outside to indicate that this is a hotel" — which is either the least effective marketing decision in Soho or the smartest one, depending on who you ask at 2am.

I've worked enough of these buildings to know which old houses are playing dress-up as "heritage" and which ones actually are the thing. Hazlitt's is the second kind. The building on the corner dates to 1718, and it is not a reproduction of a Georgian house — it's the genuine article, stitched together from three separate townhouses that got knocked through into one address at some point in their three-hundred-year run. The essayist William Hazlitt lived and died here, in 1830, in the front house, and there's a blue plaque by the door confirming it in the flat, unglamorous language English Heritage uses for these things. The hotel took his name and, more usefully, took the names of people he actually knew — his rooms are named for figures from his circle rather than for whichever influencer last checked in, which tells you something about who runs this place and what they think a hotel like this owes its history.

## What the age of the building actually means for you

Here's the part the brochure copy tends to soften and I won't: **there is no lift.** Not "limited lift access," not "lift to most floors" — none, in any of the three houses, because you cannot retrofit a Georgian townhouse without gutting the thing that makes it worth staying in. Reviewers across the board — Mr & Mrs Smith's own write-up among them — describe the same details I'd tell you myself if you asked me at the desk: creaky stairs, narrow flights, and a "wonky landing at the top" that one reviewer correctly attributed to three hundred years of the building's timber slowly settling under its own weight. If you're carrying heavy luggage, travelling with a bad knee, or need step-free access, ask for a lower floor when you book and say so plainly — a townhouse hotel this old can't fix its own bones, but a decent front desk can put you somewhere the stairs matter less.

What you get in exchange for the stairs is a building that has never been flattened into a chain's idea of "boutique." Thirty rooms, no two laid out the same, because you can't force identical floorplans onto three houses built before anyone standardized anything — claw-footed baths, four-poster beds, panelling that's actually old rather than pattern-booked, and reportedly north of two thousand paintings and prints scattered through the place rather than warehoused in one gallery wall. One guest review mentioned a resident cat, Sir Godfrey, and an honesty bar in the library, which is the kind of detail that either charms you completely or tells you this isn't your hotel — and that's a fair trade for Hazlitt's to make, because it isn't trying to be everyone's hotel. It's trying to be a specific, slightly creaky, thoroughly lived-in kind of hotel, and by most independent accounts it succeeds at that on its own terms.

The thin-walls-versus-thick-walls question that matters for most old townhouse conversions turns out to go the other way here — at least one reviewer staying three floors up reported not being able to hear Soho's Friday-night noise at all once the door was shut, which for a hotel two streets from some of the loudest bars in London is worth knowing if you're sound-sensitive and were bracing for the opposite. I'd still say check with whoever's on the desk about which rooms sit over anything noisy — an old building's soundproofing is never uniform floor to floor, whatever one happy guest's third-floor room managed.

## The honest caveats, staff-side

I haven't worked this house myself, so take this paragraph for what it is: a compiling of what guests and reviewers have actually reported, not a first-hand account. Rates have been quoted from roughly £329 a night upward, which puts it solidly in independent-boutique territory rather than budget — you're paying for the building and the history as much as the bed. Nobody I found flagged it as unwelcoming to any particular kind of guest, but nobody flagged an explicit, tested-in-practice door policy either, so I won't invent one. What's consistently true across every source is the trade this hotel is offering: no lift, no sign, no chain-hotel sameness, and in exchange a genuinely 300-year-old building that hasn't been sanded down into a theme. If that's a fair trade for you, Frith Street is a good address to buzz into. If a flight or two of narrow Georgian stairs is a dealbreaker, this isn't the house — and better you hear that from the porter than find out with a suitcase in hand at midnight.

One last thing, from the desk. A hotel that sells silence has an obvious failure mode: the night the silence stops working. Nobody plans for that. Worth knowing the address anyway.
