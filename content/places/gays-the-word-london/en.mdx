Every listing scanner in London mentions this shop for one week in February and one week in June, then forgets it exists. That's exactly backwards. The listing that matters is the one that's true in November: **the UK's oldest LGBT+ bookshop is open Tuesday through Sunday, and it needs customers, not anniversaries.**

The facts have teeth. [Gay's the Word opened on 17 January 1979](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gay%27s_the_Word_(bookshop)), founded by members of Gay Icebreakers, a gay socialist group — and if that name pings something for Near readers, it should: Icebreakers are the same collective that held disco residencies in 1976 at the Wharfdale Road pub now called <NearLink slug="central-station-kings-cross-london">Central Station</NearLink>. The people organising queer London's nights out were the people who decided it also needed somewhere to read. Same movement, two addresses, both still standing. That is not a coincidence; that is infrastructure.

## Operation Tiger, and why the name still matters

In 1984, HM Customs and Excise raided the shop in a large-scale action they named "Operation Tiger" — thousands of pounds of stock seized, and the directors charged with conspiracy to import indecent books under the Customs Consolidation Act of 1876. Read that year again: a Victorian statute, deployed against a bookshop, within living memory of anyone now in their sixties.

The defence fund raised over £55,000. Gore Vidal put in £3,000. The charges were dropped in 1986, which is the polite way of saying the state picked a fight with a bookshop and lost. The stock Customs considered indecent included, by the standard accounts, exactly the kind of literature you can now find on any chain's two shelves of February display — which is the entire argument for why the shop that fought for it deserves the sale instead.

## The back room where Pride happened

The same years, same address: **Lesbians and Gays Support the Miners** met here through 1984-85, collecting money for striking miners' families until the meetings outgrew the room. If you've seen the film *Pride*, you know the story. One honest correction the anniversary write-ups skip: the shop in the film was a set on Kingsgate Road in West Hampstead — the real one is here, smaller, and stocked.

Mark Ashton, who co-founded LGSM, is commemorated by a plaque at this address. The shop [still sells LGSM t-shirts](https://www.gaystheword.co.uk/), which is the correct way to sell history: as something you carry out the door, not something behind glass.

## What it actually is now

A working bookshop, roughly one room, dense the way only a specialist can be dense: fiction, poetry, theory, graphic novels, young adult, kids' books, biography, rare and second-hand — the deep stock a chain's algorithm will never surface, chosen by people who know why a particular out-of-print memoir matters. Author events, launches and panel discussions run through the year. Hours at time of writing: **Tuesday to Saturday 11–6, Sunday 1–6, closed Monday** — check before a special trip, as with anything on Near.

And the part that keeps this from being a heritage plaque: it nearly went under in 2007, squeezed by rent and online book-buying, and survived because people showed up — a campaign, press coverage, and then the only thing that actually saves a shop, which is sales. A bookshop that has outlived a Customs raid, a rent crisis and four decades of "print is dead" is not asking for your sympathy. It's asking what you're reading next.

## Why go

Because Bloomsbury is ten minutes' walk from the stations everyone arrives at, and this is the best possible first stop for a queer reader landing in London: you leave with a book, a free events flyer, and better recommendations than any app. And because the through-line it anchors — Icebreakers to <NearLink slug="central-station-kings-cross-london">Central Station</NearLink>, LGSM to *Pride*, 1979 to now — is the same one running through <NearLink slug="dalston-superstore-london">Dalston Superstore</NearLink> and the rest of Near's queer London coverage: institutions survive when their people treat them as necessities, not novelties.
