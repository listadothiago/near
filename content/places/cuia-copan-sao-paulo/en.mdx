Cuia sits inside a bookshop, which is the first thing to understand about it and the thing most write-ups bury. It occupies part of <NearLink slug="megafauna-copan-sao-paulo">Livraria Megafauna</NearLink> on the ground floor of Edifício Copan, Oscar Niemeyer's great curving residential slab on Avenida Ipiranga, and you eat between the shelves, in front of the open kitchen, or out in the building's ground-floor air. That arrangement is not a gimmick and not a pop-up — it opened in 2021 and has been there since — but it does set expectations correctly. This is not a quiet dedicated dining room. People browse past your table. That's the trade, and for most of the people who love the place, it's the appeal rather than the cost.

The kitchen belongs to Bel Coelho, who by the time she opened here had already run Sabuji, then Dui — the restaurant known for its Orixás menus — and Clandestino, which closed in 2020 — though she brought it back in 2024 as [Clandestina](https://www.cnnbrasil.com.br/viagemegastronomia/gastronomia/onde-os-chefs-comem-com-bel-coelho-do-cuia-e-do-clandestina-em-sp/), in Vila Madalena. Cuia reads as a distillation of what she'd been arguing for across all of them: Brazilian ingredients treated as a serious pantry rather than as regional colour. The [MICHELIN Guide](https://guide.michelin.com/us/en/sao-paulo-region/sao-paulo/restaurant/cuia) lists it, which is worth pausing on, because the guide is not in the habit of listing café counters inside shops. What it describes is all-day eating that runs from a toasted sandwich or pão de queijo up to properly composed plates — mushroom rice with tucupi and turmeric, baião de dois with smoked squash and coalho cheese — with the majority of the produce coming from agroecological or organic growers.

The name is the format. A cuia is a gourd bowl, and much of the menu arrives in one: a base, something slow-cooked or raw over it, a sauce, something with crunch. Ailin Aleixo's [Vai Se Food write-up](https://www.vaisefood.com/aonde-ir/comidinhas/cuia-o-restaurante-cafe-de-bel-coelho-no-copan/), from shortly after it opened, catalogues the early menu in detail — roasted beet with feta, baru nuts and basil; a cold bifum noodle salad with shiitake; yoghurt cake with pitanga syrup — and the shape of the thing has held since, even as individual dishes have rotated. The coffee comes from a named roaster rather than a generic house blend, and the drinks list runs to kombuchas, fresh juices, and wine by the glass rather than a full bar programme. Prices quoted in that 2021 piece are five years stale now and shouldn't be planned around; go expecting central-São Paulo café-restaurant money, not bookshop-café money.

Two honest caveats. The first is hours: the sources that list them don't agree with each other on the exact opening time, and the second Cuia that has since opened in Pinheiros muddies search results further. What every listing does agree on is the pattern — closed Mondays, open late Friday and Saturday, Sunday daytime only — so check the day you're going rather than trusting a number you read somewhere, this page included. The second is the building. Copan's ground-floor "internal street" is not signposted the way a shopping arcade would be, and Cuia is inside another business rather than behind its own street frontage, so finding it the first time takes a phone map and some willingness to wander. That's the same friction that applies to <NearLink slug="bar-fel-copan-sao-paulo">Fel</NearLink>, the small cocktail counter a few doors along, and it's why the two work well as one evening: both reward already knowing where you're going, and both are easier to reach the second time.

What makes it worth the trouble is that Cuia is doing something genuinely uncommon for a room in this price bracket. Cooking almost entirely from Brazilian ingredients is easy to claim and expensive to actually do, because it means sourcing from small growers rather than a single distributor. That the result is a lunch you can have between bookshelves, on a weekday, without booking a month out, is the whole point of the place.
