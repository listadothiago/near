Start with the frame, because the frame is the argument. A Methodist chapel is a specific kind of structure — load-bearing walls built to hold a congregation still, a nave designed to point attention one direction, toward a pulpit. Studio Voltaire took that shell in 1999 and repointed it: same walls, same volume, different vector. Where the building used to organize bodies around a sermon, it now organizes them around whatever's hung, built, or planted inside it. That's not a metaphor I'm reaching for. It's structural fact, and it's the cleanest way to understand what this place is.

## Two moves, five years apart

The organization itself predates the building. In 1994, a small collective of artists set up Studio Voltaire on Voltaire Road, Clapham — the kind of name-the-street-you're-on origin story that tells you exactly how unglamorous the beginning was. No mission-statement branding, just artists needing space and making one. Five years later, in 1999, the operation moved to its current address, 1A Nelsons Row, into the former Methodist chapel it still occupies. That's the whole load-bearing history in two sentences, and everything since has been built on top of it: a non-profit, artist-run model that channels its energy into exhibitions, commissions, residencies, and public programmes centered on emerging and under-represented artists, rather than the safer, better-collateralized names a commercial gallery would default to.

The building itself got a proper structural pass in February 2021, when a refurbished, transformed HQ reopened with work by Anthea Hamilton and William Scott — the kind of reopening show that functions as a mission statement in physical form.

## Two things that don't come down

Most gallery programming is temporary by design — that's the whole point of a "what's on" page, and I'll get to why that matters below. But Studio Voltaire has committed to two permanent installations, and permanence is the tell that separates a venue's actual identity from its rotating content.

The first is Anthea Hamilton's sculptural garden — Hamilton is a current studio holder here, not a guest artist parachuted in for a show. She designed the piece as an "edible landscape," pulling from the vernacular gardens of South London: tactile materials, seasonal planting, a design built for people to touch and use rather than admire from behind a rope. It's landscape architecture doing the job a lot of galleries ask painting to do — making a case for how a community actually occupies space.

The second is the work of Joanne Tatham and Tom O'Sullivan, who've collaborated with Studio Voltaire since 2004 — two decades of an ongoing relationship, which in the art world counts as a marriage. Their permanent piece, *The Institute For The Magical Effect Of Actually Giving A Shit (a note to our future self)*, is a hand-glazed ceramic tile installation on the building's public-facing surface, working through playful motifs to transform what a passerby sees before they've even decided to walk in. A chapel facade, retiled by artists, addressed to nobody in particular and everybody who passes. That's zine logic applied to architecture: cheap materials, direct address, made to be seen by people who didn't buy a ticket.

## How a scrappy space ends up working with LOEWE

Somewhere around Studio Voltaire's 30th anniversary, the fashion house LOEWE came calling for a collaboration — the kind of pairing that could, in the wrong hands, read as a non-profit selling its credibility for sponsorship money. It didn't play that way here, because the structure underneath — artist-run, non-profit, chapel walls, permanent Hamilton and Tatham/O'Sullivan installations — was never for sale. A major fashion house showing up doesn't rebuild a space's identity; it just means enough people noticed the identity was already load-bearing.

## What's actually on

Here's where I have to be precise instead of evocative: right now, Christine Hazell's *Different Faces* is on (15 July–18 October 2026) — Hazell took up drawing at 88 after memory loss reshaped how she worked, and the show is exactly the kind of under-represented, unlikely-CV artist this venue's whole model is built to champion. It overlaps with the run-up to Katherine Hubbard's *The Great Room*, opening 7 October and running to 10 January 2027, which turns the gallery into a labyrinth of partition walls referencing the makeshift bedroom Hubbard's mother built inside her own Philadelphia living room after a dementia diagnosis — a five-year project about viewership and care, developed alongside an extended chapter at Vienna's Secession. Two earlier shows are already closed for the record: Jack Scollard's *Cruising Archaeology: The Pleasure Archive Research Centre* (22 April–5 July 2026) and a T. Venkanna show (17 May–23 August 2026). Gallery programming turns over on its own schedule, faster than any static page can track, so check [studiovoltaire.org/whats-on/](https://studiovoltaire.org/whats-on/) before you go — that's not a hedge, that's the only honest way to send you there. Exact opening hours aren't confirmed here either; the site has the current word.

Go for the chapel. Go for the permanent garden and the tiled facade, which will be there regardless of what's hanging inside. What's inside changes. The structure doesn't.
