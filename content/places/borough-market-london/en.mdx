There's been a market on or near this site since at least 1276, when it's first recorded as trading on London Bridge itself before being pushed south onto Borough High Street. That's the detail every Borough Market write-up leads with, and it's worth leading with again, because it changes how the place should actually be read: this isn't a heritage-themed food hall built to evoke an old market. It's the market, continuously, through relocations, a 19th-century rebuild under Victorian iron-and-glass train sheds, a near-death period in the mid-20th century when wholesale trade nearly killed it off, and a 1990s revival that turned it into the retail destination it is now.

That history shows up in the layout, which doesn't resolve into a single clean hall the way newer food markets do. Borough sprawls across several connected sections — Green Market, Jubilee Market, the Floral Hall structure salvaged from a demolished Covent Garden building — stitched together under train tracks that still run trains overhead on the working line into London Bridge station. Southwark Cathedral sits at one entrance, close enough that market noise and church bells overlap most mornings. None of it was designed as a single coherent experience, because none of it was designed at once; it accumulated.

The stalls skew toward specialists rather than generalists: a cheesemonger that does nothing but cheese (Neal's Yard Dairy gets singled out by [Time Out's current guide](https://www.timeout.com/london/things-to-do/borough-market)), a butcher that's been on the same pitch for generations, the Black Pig's Italian sandwiches, Bread Ahead's doughnuts, a scotch egg counter with a genuine cult following, oyster stands, a spice merchant whose stall alone is worth the detour for the smell. This is still, underneath the tourist traffic, a working food market — wholesale trading happens before the market opens to the public, and many of the stalls sell to restaurant buyers as their actual primary business, retail customers being a secondary (if now dominant) revenue stream. It's shut on Mondays (except in the run-up to Christmas), which catches first-timers out often enough to be worth flagging.

Weekday mornings before 11am are the closest thing to a quiet visit; Saturday afternoons are the opposite, closer to a football match's worth of foot traffic funneled through narrow covered lanes. Two independent write-ups make the same honest point rather than glossing over it: Time Out's own guide admits the market "can get extremely cramped and chaotically busy," and [The Guide Magazine's stall roundup](https://www.theguidemagazine.org/post/the-complete-guide-to-the-best-stalls-in-borough-market) describes being "joined by hundreds, if not thousands, of others" every visit, with "terribly long queues" and having to weave in and out of the alleyways to get anywhere. Both still rate the food highly — this isn't a case of the market coasting on reputation — but the crowding is real, not a minor caveat. There's no real seating strategy that works either — the market isn't built for it — so the standard local move is a walking lunch: a scotch egg here, a raclette sandwich there, a coffee to finish, eaten standing or perched on whatever ledge is free.
