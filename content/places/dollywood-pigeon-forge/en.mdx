Parton [died on 25 August 2026, aged 80](https://www.npr.org/2026/08/25/nx-s1-3650175/dolly-parton-obituary), after what her publicist described as a brief battle with cancer. Dollywood [has said it stays open](https://www.billboard.com/music/music-news/dollywood-open-dolly-parton-death-1236325074/) — park president Eugene Naughton addressed the question directly the day after her death, telling visitors worried that things would change without her that they wouldn't. This page was written before she died and has been corrected rather than rewritten: what follows describes a park she spent four decades shaping, and which is still open.

There's a version of a celebrity-branded theme park that's just a name on a gate, and there's Dollywood, which is neither subtle nor accidental about being the actual, hands-on project of a specific person: Dolly Parton, who co-owned the park through Dolly Parton Productions alongside Herschend, and whose presence — musically, aesthetically, in the park's own framing of itself — was baked into the place rather than licensed to it. That distinction matters more than it might sound like it should, because it shows up in the difference between a park that feels manufactured by committee and one that feels like it has an actual point of view.

The physical setting does real work here too. Dollywood sits on 160 acres inside the Great Smoky Mountains, and the terrain isn't flattened into a generic fairground — the park's better coasters use the elevation change instead of fighting it, and 2026 brings [NightFlight Expedition](https://www.dollywood.com/themepark/), a new indoor hybrid ride that combines a coaster with a whitewater-style river sequence across four linked scenes, the kind of ambitious, not-quite-classifiable attraction that a park confident in its own identity can commit to building. Existing headline rides — Lightning Rod, Wild Eagle, the wooden Thunderhead, Tennessee Tornado, Mystery Mine, Big Bear Mountain — already cover a wide enough range that "there's nothing for me here" isn't a fair complaint from most visitors, thrill-seeking or not.

Independent visitor coverage backs up the reputation: Dollywood shows up consistently near the top of [US theme park rankings](https://travel.usnews.com/Pigeon_Forge_TN/Things_To_Do/Dollywood_65198/), with reviewers specifically calling out cleanliness and cohesive theming rather than just ride count — a park that's been maintained, not just built once and left to wear down.

The honest complaints are consistent enough across independent reviews to take seriously rather than wave off. Tickets run close to double the price of a typical regional amusement park, with a separate parking fee on top — this is a real budget-line trip, not a casual afternoon stop. Wait times on popular rides can run 40 minutes on an average day and closer to two hours on a crowded one, particularly for the Dollywood Express steam train. And [a handful of reviewers](https://thewanderingwhittens.com/2025/09/18/my-honest-review-of-dollywood/) report the least forgiving version of theme-park bad luck: rides closed for weather with no refund offered for the wasted wait. None of that erases the park's real strengths, but a trip planned around off-peak timing and realistic expectations on cost will go noticeably better than one that isn't.

Pair a Dollywood day with the wider Pigeon Forge and Gatlinburg corridor if the trip allows more than one day — the park is the anchor of that whole stretch of the Smokies' tourist economy, not an isolated stop.
