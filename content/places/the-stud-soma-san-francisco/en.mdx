Most bars that call themselves "historic" mean the neon sign is old. The Stud means it structurally: when the previous owner couldn't absorb a rent increase in 2016, the people who actually worked there — bartenders, DJs, drag performers, door staff — bought the business themselves. Seventeen of them went in on it. Collective member Nate Allbee called it the first worker-owned co-op nightclub in the country, and nobody's seriously disputed that since. The bar itself dates to 1966, opened by George Matson and Alexis Muir, which makes it San Francisco's oldest continuously-branded gay bar even with the ownership overhaul and the address changes along the way.

And there have been address changes. The Stud started at 1535 Folsom Street, moved to 9th and Harrison in 1987, then closed in May 2020 when the pandemic shutdown made the math impossible. It came back April 20, 2024, at a genuinely new location — [1123 Folsom Street](https://www.kqed.org/arts/13956246/the-stud-san-francisco-lgbtq-bar-reopening), a few blocks from where it used to be. The 9th & Harrison building is gone from the business entirely; it's not a "same room, new coat of paint" reopening. Worth flagging here since the most findable photo of this place online — a Creative Commons shot on Wikimedia Commons, which is what's running as this page's hero image — was taken outside that old 9th & Harrison building in 2019, a year before it closed. It's a real, honestly-sourced photo of The Stud, just not of the building currently open at 1123 Folsom. We're running it captioned as such rather than pretending otherwise or reaching for a stock photo of an unrelated bar.

The two 2026 stories worth actually understanding, and worth not conflating with each other:

The Instagram suspension was real, fast, and resolved fast. In late July 2026, right before Dore Alley weekend — one of the collective's biggest revenue windows of the year — someone filed false reports flagging The Stud's old party posts as "human trafficking." Meta suspended the account, cutting off a page co-owner Rachel Ryan said doubled as the bar's marketing, ticketing, and communications system in one. After [public pressure from State Senator Scott Wiener and Supervisor Matt Dorsey](https://missionlocal.org/2026/07/soma-queer-gay-bar-meta-suspended-instagram-hate-homophobia/), Meta restored the account with its 32,000 followers intact the same day. It's a clean, sourced example of what a coordinated false-report campaign can do to a small LGBTQ+ business that runs its whole operation through one platform account.

"Stud Alley" is a different thing entirely, and the name similarity does a lot of unearned work in casual conversation. It's an unsanctioned, anonymously organized free party that's popped up during Pride for the past several years in the alley next to The Stud's old, now-closed 9th & Harrison building — not an event The Stud collective throws or is affiliated with. In 2026, [SFPD in riot gear shut it down](https://www.kalw.org/bay-area-news/2026-08-25/stud-alley-defendants-have-arraignment-postponed-to-next-week), citing a failure to disperse; 20 people were arrested, 18 now facing misdemeanor charges including unlawful assembly and obstruction. Defense attorneys dispute that a dispersal order was ever given and argue this was a dance party, not a protest — that dispute is still working through arraignment as of late August 2026. None of that is a story about the actual working bar at 1123 Folsom; it's a story about an unrelated street gathering that borrowed its name.

If you're going: The Stud still runs the kind of calendar you'd expect from a bar that survived on community ownership — drag, DJ nights, themed parties, the occasional throwback to "different eras" of its own 60-year run. Check studsf.com before a specific night, since a co-op-run venue with a recently-restored social account is exactly the kind of place where the calendar is more current than any single article.
