Get the address right first, because half the internet doesn't: Rush Hour is at **Spuistraat 110**, not 116. It moved there over the weekend of 21 March 2022 — three doors down from the spot it had held for about six years — and some directories never caught up. The move wasn't a downsize or a rebrand; cofounder Antal called it a chance to finally hold a basement, and the shop now runs across two levels, a ground floor redone with what Resident Advisor described as a "living room feel" and a basement that gets used when things get busy, or when there's a show.

## A mail-order list that grew into a label

Rush Hour started in late 1997 the unglamorous way — a shop and mail-order service importing house and techno records that weren't easy to find anywhere else in Amsterdam. By 1999 the operation had grown a distribution wing, and just before the new millennium, that turned into an in-house label. It's the kind of trajectory that sounds inevitable in hindsight and wasn't: most record shops that started as import operations in the late nineties didn't survive the vinyl collapse of the mid-2000s, let alone come out the other side running a globally respected label. Rush Hour did both, and did it from a shop on one of central Amsterdam's busiest shopping streets rather than from some warehouse on the edge of town.

## The roster that made it a scene, not a store

What separates Rush Hour from a well-curated record shop is the label attached to it. Hunee, Young Marco, and Interstellar Funk all came up through it; Tom Trago and Awanto 3 are part of the Amsterdam contingent it's put on the map internationally. San Proper's loyalty runs deep enough that he has the Rush Hour logo tattooed on him — not the kind of thing you do for a shop you just buy records from. Maybe the clearest proof of the label's ear, though, is what it did for Soichi Terada: a Japanese house producer whose original records from the eighties and nineties were largely unheard outside Japan until Rush Hour's reissue and compilation work put him back in front of an international audience, kicking off a genuine late-career renaissance. They've also released Ryuichi Sakamoto and Ron Trent — not names that show up on a label without a real reputation behind it.

## What the shop floor actually feels like

Walk in and it doesn't read as a museum piece trading on its own history, which a 25-plus-year-old record shop easily could. The 2022 redesign leaned into an open, low-key layout up front — crates you can actually flip through without elbowing a stranger, a listening station, daylight through the tall windows a Spuistraat canal house comes with — and kept the basement for when the shop needs the extra floor: peak Saturday crowds, Amsterdam Dance Event season, or an actual in-store. That basement is where **Lex Records brings Lord Jah-Monte Ogbon and Vayda for a live in-store on Thursday, 3 September 2026** — the kind of low-ceiling, close-quarters set that a record shop basement does better than any club, because there's no barrier between the artist and forty people who specifically walked in for this.

If De Trut, over in Oud-West, is what an Amsterdam institution looks like when it runs entirely on volunteers and refuses to be a business at all, <NearLink slug="de-trut-amsterdam">De Trut</NearLink> and Rush Hour still share the same underlying logic: neither one built its reputation by chasing what club culture wanted this month. One did it in a squatted basement with a €3 door; the other did it importing records nobody else in the city was stocking and eventually starting a label to put its own name behind the sound. Different rooms, same refusal to be generic about it.
