# Near.tips SEO + GEO Strategy Report

### September 4, 2026

## Executive summary

My blunt assessment:

> **Near has a much better SEO idea than it currently has an SEO footprint.**

The product concept is unusually well suited to search:

- real places
    
- real locations
    
- real events
    
- neighborhoods
    
- local intent
    
- highly specific tastes
    
- multilingual content
    
- structured place data
    
- editorial curation
    
- human/source provenance
    
- maps
    
- potentially useful evergreen + time-sensitive content
    

And importantly, the concept isn't simply another “best restaurants in London” site.

Near describes itself as **“the alternative guide to everywhere”**, explicitly positioning against generic tourism and toward counterculture/alternative local discovery. ([Near Tips](https://near.tips/en/about "About Near | near.tips"))

That's the moat.

The problem is that Google doesn't currently have much evidence that Near is an authority on anything yet.

So I would organize the next 12–18 months around:

### 1. Establish topical authority

Not “cover the whole world.”

**Own specific combinations of:**

> city × neighborhood × taste × place/event

### 2. Build search-indexable location architecture

The backlog is already moving in the right direction here.

### 3. Build an enormous long-tail surface

Not by creating 10,000 junk pages.

By creating **excellent entity pages and tightly curated location/collection pages** that naturally capture hundreds of related queries.

### 4. Make Near exceptionally extractable by AI

Not through “GEO tricks.”

Through **clear facts, entity relationships, dates, sources, distinctive editorial judgment and structured content**.

Google's current guidance explicitly says the same SEO fundamentals underpin its generative search features, and specifically recommends unique, non-commodity content and useful local content. ([Google for Developers](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide?authuser=4&hl=en&utm_source=chatgpt.com "Google's Guide to Optimizing for Generative AI Features on Google Search | Google Search Central  |  Documentation  |  Google for Developers"))

### 5. Use Trends as a _radar_, not a keyword-volume oracle

This is particularly important.

Google says Trends is normalized relative data, not absolute search volume. It also distinguishes literal search terms from broader topics. ([Google Help](https://support.google.com/trends/answer/17309543?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Compare search terms and topics - Trends Help"))

Your backlog's existing rule — **trend → test against Near's editorial angle → only then act** — is exactly right.

# 13. Current Google Trends reality check

I checked Google's live Trending Now surfaces for:

- UK
    
- Brazil
    
- US
    
- Italy
    
- Spain
    
- Mexico
    

and specifically examined the seven-day versions corresponding to the feeds in the backlog.

Important technical finding:

### Google is currently rendering these URLs as **All categories**, despite the `category=19` parameter appearing in the URL.

For example, the UK feed URL resolves in the rendered interface as:

> United Kingdom → Past 7 days → **All categories**

rather than visibly applying category 19. ([Google Trends](https://trends.google.com/trending?category=19&geo=GB&hours=168 "Trending Now - Google Trends"))

Same behavior is visible for Brazil and the other markets. ([Google Trends](https://trends.google.com/trending?category=19&geo=BR&hours=168 "Trending Now - Google Trends"))

So I would **not pretend that I obtained a clean category-19 travel dataset from those URLs**.

That's actually useful information for your implementation.

Google's documentation confirms that Trending Now supports location, timeframe, category filtering and RSS export, and is refreshed roughly every ten minutes. ([Google Help](https://support.google.com/trends/answer/3076011?hl=en&utm_source=chatgpt.com "Explore the searches that are Trending now - Trends Help"))

# 21. Entity SEO

This is a huge opportunity.

Every good Near place page should be able to answer:

### What is it?

### Where is it?

### What neighborhood?

### What city?

### What kind of place?

### Why does Near care?

### Who is it for?

### When is it open?

### Is it currently active?

### What events happen there?

### What sources verify it?

### What nearby things relate to it?

That is an **entity card for Google and LLMs**, not just an article.

Google explicitly recommends using the most specific applicable `LocalBusiness` subtype and keeping structured data representative of the visible content. ([Google for Developers](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/local-business?authuser=77&utm_source=chatgpt.com "Local Business (LocalBusiness) Structured Data | Google Search Central  |  Documentation  |  Google for Developers"))

So:

> Restaurant

rather than generic:

> LocalBusiness

when appropriate.

# 22. Structured data roadmap

I'd prioritize:

### Place pages

`Restaurant`, `BarOrPub`, `Hotel`, `HealthClub`, etc. where applicable.

### Events

`Event`

### Editorial

`Article` / appropriate article subtype

### Breadcrumbs

`BreadcrumbList`

### Person

Only genuine people.

This last point is important because Near's current architecture explicitly refuses to pretend its AI personas are human people. That's the correct decision.

Google's structured-data guidance says markup must represent visible content and misleading structured data can cause manual action. ([Google for Developers](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/sd-policies?utm_source=chatgpt.com "General Structured Data Guidelines | Google Search Central  |  Documentation  |  Google for Developers"))

# 43. The anti-listicle rule is an SEO advantage

This sounds counterintuitive.

But it is.

Generic AI content can produce:

That is much harder.

And Google's current generative-search guidance specifically emphasizes **non-commodity content and unique viewpoints**. ([Google for Developers](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide?authuser=4&hl=en&utm_source=chatgpt.com "Google's Guide to Optimizing for Generative AI Features on Google Search | Google Search Central  |  Documentation  |  Google for Developers"))

So your editorial weirdness isn't an obstacle to SEO.

**It's the SEO strategy.**
