# External strategy analysis — Near's three-year SEO/GEO opportunity

**Source:** ChatGPT, delivered to the operator 2026-09-02. Verbatim, saved as a
local reference. **Not yet Near policy** — it is input for a joint review by the
Product Trio, near-seo, aeo, near-trendsetter and the Chief Editor (see BACKLOG).

Site state at time of writing: 58 places indexed, 107 sources watched.

---

Yes. I looked at the live site rather than treating this as a hypothetical SEO project, and I think **Near has a genuinely interesting three-year opportunity**.

The important bit is that I would *not* turn it into a generic "things to do in X" SEO farm. That would put you into a brutally competitive category and, more importantly, destroy what is currently distinctive about the project.

Right now Near describes itself as **"the alternative guide to everywhere"**, with an alt-weekly/counterculture positioning. The site currently has only **58 places indexed and 107 sources watched**, so you're essentially at day zero from an SEO perspective. ([near.tips][1])

And that's actually good news.

# My thesis for Near

I would build it as:

> **A machine-assisted, human-directed local intelligence layer for places that mainstream travel guides don't understand very well.**

Not Yelp.

Not TripAdvisor.

Not Time Out.

Not "10 best restaurants in São Paulo."

Something more like:

**"What is actually interesting around here, right now?"**

That positioning gives you a very nice SEO/GEO wedge.

And there's a particularly important timing advantage: Google has just rolled out dedicated reporting for visibility in AI Overviews and AI Mode to websites globally as of August 31, 2026. You can now see which pages are appearing in Google's generative search features. ([Google Help][2])

So we're not just optimizing for ten blue links anymore.

We're optimizing for:

**Search → AI answer → Near citation → Near visit.**

---

# First: what I like about the site

I actually think you've accidentally stumbled onto several things that are strategically good.

The homepage already has:

* Tips
* Guides
* categories/columns
* sources
* geographical context
* freshness
* editorial bylines
* individual places
* maps
* multiple languages
* a distinctive editorial voice

And the AI bylines are unusually transparent.

The About page explicitly says that every byline is AI, identifies the different editorial personalities, and says the underlying reporting comes from real sources. ([near.tips][3])

That's **much better than pretending an AI wrote it all magically**.

Google explicitly says AI-generated content isn't inherently a problem; the danger is producing lots of pages with little original value primarily to manipulate rankings. ([Google for Developers][4])

So your model can work.

But there's a catch:

## The AI cannot become the product.

The **information architecture + data + sourcing + editorial judgment** have to be the product.

AI is the production machinery.

That's a very different thing.

It will be combinations like:

> underground music venues near me

> queer things to do in Santos

And then increasingly specific combinations:

> **LGBTQ+ nightlife in São Paulo that isn't a club**

> **quiet bars in London for people who hate cocktail bars**

> **places to stay in Brighton if you want queer nightlife**

That is where Near can become a **semantic web of local intent**.

# And I would go MUCH further than individual place pages

This is the biggest strategic recommendation I'd make.

Think of Near as having **four content layers**.

### Layer 1 — Places

The atomic unit.

```text
near.tips/en/places/the-stud-san-francisco
near.tips/en/places/lita-sao-paulo
near.tips/en/places/...
```

Each place gets:

* what it is
* why it matters
* neighborhood
* city
* category
* audience
* vibe
* price
* opening hours
* current status
* sources
* nearby places
* related guides
* editorial history
* last verified date

This is your **database disguised as editorial content**.

---

# Layer 2 — Neighborhood/city pages

This is where organic traffic starts getting interesting.

For example:

### `/en/sao-paulo/pinheiros`

Could eventually answer:

> What's interesting in Pinheiros?

And contain:

* bars
* restaurants

Google gets a strong entity:

**Pinheiros → São Paulo → places → categories → guides → sources**

That's much more powerful than 30 disconnected articles.

# Layer 3 — Intent pages

This is the real SEO gold.

Instead of merely:

> São Paulo nightlife

you want:

> São Paulo nightlife for...

* queer people
* alternative people
* solo travelers
* people who don't drink
* people over 40
* cheap nights out
* live music
* experimental music
* dates
* first dates
* rainy days
* Sunday nights
* late nights
* people who hate clubs
* people who want conversation
* etc.

Some of these will be editorial guides.

Some can eventually be dynamically assembled from your database.

But **don't automatically create 100,000 combinations**.

Google specifically warns against mass-producing pages primarily for search traffic. ([Google for Developers][5])

Instead:

> identify combinations where there is genuine user intent + enough interesting places + something genuinely useful to say.

# Layer 4 — Editorial guides

This is where Near can build authority and links.

Your existing guides are already pointing in this direction.

For example:

> **"Asian Food Was Never One Thing"**

That's much more interesting than:

> "3 Best Asian Restaurants in São Paulo."

And:

> **"The Zombie Listicle Problem"**

is even more interesting strategically.

It's not just SEO content.

It's **Near developing an editorial worldview**.

That's valuable.

---

# The killer content loop

Here's the system I'd build.

**Source → Place → Observation → Guide → Neighborhood → City → Search query**

For example:

A source mentions an interesting bar.

↓

Near creates a place.

↓

The place belongs to:

`São Paulo → Pinheiros → wine bars`

↓

You discover five other interesting places.

↓

Near publishes:

> **5 places in Pinheiros that don't feel like Pinheiros**

↓

That guide links to all five.

↓

Each place links back to the guide.

↓

The Pinheiros page aggregates them.

↓

São Paulo page aggregates neighborhoods.

↓

Google discovers an increasingly coherent topical graph.

That's **much more defensible than blogging.**

---

# Your secret weapon: freshness

This could be enormous.

Local information decays.

Restaurants close.

Bars change names.

Hotels change ownership.

Clubs disappear.

Events move.

Instagram accounts die.

Google Maps listings remain mysteriously undead for years.

Near already has the concept of **sources watched + last sync**. ([near.tips][1])

I'd lean *hard* into this.

Eventually every place should have something like:

> **Last checked: 2 September 2026**

And perhaps:

> **Status: Active**

or

> **Last verified 14 days ago**

That creates a fundamentally different proposition from static travel content.

---

# This is where GEO gets interesting

Google's current guidance is actually quite encouraging for you.

Google says there isn't a special "AI SEO" markup required for AI Overviews or AI Mode. Normal SEO fundamentals still apply: crawlability, internal links, good page experience, textual content, accurate structured data, etc. ([Google for Developers][6])

In other words:

**Don't build an "AI SEO hack."**

Build a very good information system.

Then make it incredibly easy for machines to understand.

---

# The GEO architecture I'd use

Every place page should answer obvious questions in plain language.

For example:

### The Stud

**What is The Stud?**

A worker-owned queer nightclub in San Francisco...

**Where is The Stud?**

SoMa, San Francisco...

**What is it known for?**

...

**Who is it good for?**

...

**What kind of nightlife is it?**

...

**Is The Stud still open?**

...

**What is nearby?**

...

**Sources**

...

That gives LLMs incredibly clean retrieval targets.

Google specifically says AI features use the existing Search index and that crawlable, indexable pages are eligible to become supporting links. ([Google for Developers][6])

Then every mention of that place points toward the canonical page.

You're essentially constructing your own **small local knowledge graph**.

That's much more exciting than "blog SEO."

# Three-year roadmap

Here's roughly how I'd think about the next 12 quarters.

## Q4 2026 — Foundation

**Goal: make Near indexable and coherent.**

Don't chase traffic yet.

Get:

* canonical URLs
* XML sitemap
* robots.txt
* Search Console
* GA4 or equivalent
* structured data
* clean titles/descriptions
* OpenGraph
* canonical place entities
* city/neighborhood hierarchy
* internal linking
* proper pagination
* fast pages
* SSR/static HTML wherever practical
* multilingual URL architecture

And get your first:

### 100–300 genuinely good places.

Not 10,000 crap ones.

**500–1,000 places**

# 2029 Q1–Q2 — Search beyond Google

This is where GEO becomes much more interesting.

You want someone asking:

# Q3–Q4 2029 — Monetisation

This is where I'd get serious about revenue.

Not necessarily sooner.

Because ads on 300 pages with 30 visitors each are basically decorative confetti.

Once you've got significant traffic, you've got several options.

## 1. Display advertising

The boring but easy one.

Google's own metric is Page RPM:

> estimated earnings / pageviews × 1,000. ([Google Help][7])

So if, hypothetically, Near eventually got:

| Monthly pageviews |  $5 RPM | $10 RPM |
| ----------------: | ------: | ------: |
|               50k |    $250 |    $500 |
|              100k |    $500 |  $1,000 |
|              500k |  $2,500 |  $5,000 |
|                1M |  $5,000 | $10,000 |
|                5M | $25,000 | $50,000 |

Those are **illustrative scenarios, not forecasts**. Actual RPM varies enormously by country, audience, season, ad demand, device, layout, and traffic quality.

For a hobby business, though?

Even **$500/month** is already "hey, this weird little machine pays for itself."

# What I would NOT do

This is important.

### ❌ Don't become "AI TripAdvisor"

Huge competitive surface.

### ❌ Don't publish 100,000 AI articles

Google explicitly warns about scaled content created primarily for rankings. ([Google for Developers][4])

### ❌ Don't chase every city

Density beats breadth initially.

### ❌ Don't obsess over keyword volume

Your best queries may have tiny apparent volume.

A query with 100 searches/month and a 60% CTR can be more valuable than a 50,000-volume keyword where you'll never rank.

### ❌ Don't make every page "Top 10…"

Your current voice is considerably more interesting than that.

**$500–$3k/month**

# My 3-year north star

If this were my hobby project, I'd put this on the wall:

> **By September 2029, Near should know 10,000–50,000 interesting places across 50+ cities, know why each one is interesting, know where the information came from, know roughly how fresh that information is, and be one of the sources AI assistants trust when someone asks "what's actually worth doing around here?"**

That's ambitious without being Silicon Valley delusional.

And there's one particularly nice aspect of the timing:

**you don't need to beat Google at search.**

You need to become one of the little pieces of the web that Google's systems, other LLMs, and actual humans find unusually useful.

Google itself says AI Search still relies on its core Search ranking systems, and that AI features can surface a wider and more diverse set of supporting pages. ([Google for Developers][6])

That plays *very* nicely into what Near is trying to become.

## If I were you, my next 90 days would be brutally simple

**1. Fix the technical SEO foundation.**

**2. Get to ~300 excellent places.**

**3. Concentrate them in 3–5 cities.**

**4. Build the place → neighborhood → city → guide graph.**

**5. Create ~20 genuinely excellent guides.**

**6. Start collecting Search Console data immediately.**

**7. Track AI-search visibility separately.**

**8. Don't turn on ads yet.**

**9. Don't spend money on SEO tools yet.**

**10. Learn which *types of Near pages* Google actually wants.**

Then let the data tell us what to scale.

And honestly, **I wouldn't spend the next three months writing 300 blog posts. I'd spend them making the underlying Near data model ridiculously good.** That's the bit that could make this thing compound rather than just become another little content site.
